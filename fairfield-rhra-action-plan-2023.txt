ABOUT THE RURAL HOUSING READINESS ASSESSMENT (RHRA)

The Rural Housing Readiness Assessment (RHRA) is a tool that helps communities self-assess their housing needs and guides them in the decision-making process when addressing those needs. It guides communities in considering options that ensure existing and potential residents are able to find safe, secure, and quality housing that meets their needs and fits within their budgets. The RHRA helps communities know what kinds of housing-related information they should gather and review to make informed decisions. The assessment checklists aid in the creation of local housing policy that is appropriate for the needs and desires of a community.

ISU Extension and Outreach CED maintains a series of RHRA fact sheets available for download from the RHRA webpage (https://www.extension.iastate.edu/communities/rural-housing-readiness-assessment), including:

- RHRA: Open Meetings and Open Records Laws
- RHRA: Updating City Ordinances
- RHRA: Previous Housing Recommendations Worksheet
- RHRA: Low-Income Housing Tax Credits

RHRA WORKBOOK SELF-ASSESSMENT

The first step in the Rural Housing Readiness Assessment (RHRA) process is completing the RHRA workbook. The Fairfield housing committee members conducted the self-assessment, which involved gathering planning documents directly and indirectly related to housing, identifying relevant sections of city code, and compiling an inventory of existing housing programs in Fairfield (Appendix A). While reviewing the completed RHRA self-assessment workbook in advance of the first workshop, several items stood out to the CED team:

- Fairfield has a comprehensive plan, dated November 2020 and completed by the consulting firm Confluence. The plan, in part, outlines why the provision of adequate and attainable housing is a vital foundation for supporting sustainable community and economic development. Housing-related goals in this comprehensive plan include:
  - Preserve existing older, single-family homes in low-density residential neighborhoods
  - Protect the rights of renters and landlords in Fairfield
  - Increase and support housing development

- The city's code of ordinances around zoning, nuisances, and rental inspections, and the application and degree to which city officials are following those ordinances to the letter of the law is not clear; there is a concerted effort with hiring an enforcement officer to oversee and implement rental inspections according to code requirements.

- Vacant lots and buildings in Fairfield are an opportunity for growth and an obligation on the part of the city government to manage and maintain them until they are occupied again. Incentivizing their re-use, adaptation, and/or demolition could prove to be a catalytic investment in the community if done with the comprehensive plan in mind.

COMMUNITY SURVEY

CED specialists designed a community survey to capture public input on housing in Fairfield, as well as the demographic profile of survey respondents. The survey became available for distribution following the conclusion of the first workshop. Steering committee members oversaw making the survey available to community members, focusing on distribution through email lists and various media outlets. A total of 1,205 individual responses were collected, with 1,041 respondents self-identifying as Fairfield residents, which corresponds to approximately 12.5% of the city's population. In addition to collecting demographic and economic information, the survey asked respondents what they saw holding Fairfield back from making progress on improving housing and what possible improvements might look like. HOME BUYER EDUCATION PROGRAM

A home-buyer education program comes before the purchase of a home and focuses more on the financial sustainability of the purchase and its impact on the family budget. This type of program helps potential home buyers clean up any credit issues that may impact the quality of the loan for which they may qualify in the marketplace. ISU Extension and Outreach offers an online home-buyer education course called "A Place of Your Own" (https://www.extension.iastate.edu/humansciences/homebuyer). People can enroll in this six-session Moodle course at any time for $45. Those who complete the course receive a certificate that they can share with their mortgage lender or realtor.

Home, Inc. offers a similar home-buyer education program that includes elements of homeownership finance, purchasing a home, and maintaining a home. The course costs $100 and is available online (https://www.ehomeamerica.org/homeincdsm).

Some nonprofits — such as community action agencies, councils of government, or Habitat for Humanity organizations — may have their own internal home-buyer education courses required to receive assistance, or rules of the programs they are managing on behalf of the state or federal government, but these are generally NOT available to the public.

BUILD NEW HOUSING

When researching and compiling housing grants and resources for the community, it is important to become familiar with the financial resource programs available in the area, taking the time to visit the website links and read about the programs. Answers to the following questions will provide a thorough understanding of a program:

1. Who is eligible to receive the funding? Is it for homeowners or developers?
2. Who is eligible to apply for the funding? Is it an individual developer or the city on behalf of the community?
3. What are the assistance amounts? How much does the organization typically fund for a project and what percentage of a project's costs might come from this program or resource?
4. What is the process for applying for this resource? Is there an annual grant round or is funding available at any time you have a project ready to go?
5. Who is the main contact person at this organization who could answer more questions? Personal contacts with a grant officer are one of the best time investments one can make. Even if a project isn't a direct match for the program the grant officer administers, they are often able to point you in the right direction. Additionally, if your project does meet their funding criteria, they may also point you to additional funding sources that others have used to bring more money into the project.
6. What are the matching fund requirements?
7. What other projects have been funded recently and can you get a copy of their application?

STATE HOUSING TRUST FUND GRANT PROGRAM

The Iowa Finance Authority's State Housing Trust Fund Project-Based Housing Program provides assistance for housing projects that are not eligible to receive funding under a local housing trust fund's housing assistance plan (https://www.iowafinance.com/state-housing-trust-fund/project-based-housing-program/). Applications are accepted starting July 1 of the current fiscal year and are accepted until available funds are exhausted.

AHEAD REGIONAL HOUSING TRUST FUND

The Area 15 Regional Planning Commission offers a regional housing trust fund (RHTF) that supports affordable housing for low-to-moderate income households in Davis, Jefferson, Keokuk, Mahaska, Van Buren, and Wapello Counties. The Affordable Housing Enterprises and Development (AHEAD) Regional Housing Trust Fund assists individuals, community organizations, nonprofit and for-profit developers in rehabilitation, repair, and new construction projects that serve households with incomes at or below 80% of the HUD median family income guideline, with at least 30% of project dollars used to benefit extremely low-income households (less than 30% median income). Projects that address repair and rehabilitation of the region's aging housing stock are given priority (https://www.area15rpc.com/rhtf).

LOCALLY-CONSTITUTED INVESTOR GROUP / HOUSING COALITION

A locally constituted housing development investor group can crowdsource capital and reinvest it into constructing housing and land redevelopment. Local investors create community buy-in, demonstrating pride and opportunity. They can also help circulate housing development dollars to stay local, benefiting the local economy. Sioux Center is a community in northwest Iowa that has successfully developed new housing with a similar public-private partnership. Sioux Center Land Development worked with the City of Sioux Center to develop 75 new-construction housing units. In 2012, the project earned an award for innovation from the Iowa Finance Authority. A description of the project, including house plans and pricing for the types of housing they could construct, is located on the HousingIowa Conference website (https://www.housingiowaconference.com/media/cms/2012_Sioux_Center_Land_Development_2169A76A1F69E.pdf).

URBAN RENEWAL AREAS AND TAX INCREMENT FINANCING (TIF)

Tax Increment Financing (TIF) is a tool that encourages housing development. TIF permits municipalities to issue bonds or borrow money to pay for infrastructure improvements in urban renewal areas using future tax revenues that result from the improvements. Among other items, TIF funds can be used for public improvements and the repair or rehabilitation, demolition, or removal of buildings. TIF funds can even be used to relocate displaced households due to urban renewal projects, including paying moving expenses and paying rent for displaced persons for up to five years. The RHRA fact sheet on tax increment financing provides more information (Appendix E, pp. 181–182). Chapter 404 of the Iowa Code defines the rules for establishing urban revitalization tax exemptions (https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/ico/chapter/404.pdf). Urban renewal areas and TIF are complex areas of law that carry severe consequences if misused or poorly managed. It is highly recommended that an attorney specializing in these laws be consulted.

Urban renewal areas have an associated urban renewal plan that the local planning commission must approve. Among other requirements, the plan identifies how TIF funds will be spent; funds cannot be spent on items not specified in the plan. As the city evaluates its current urban renewal area, it should keep in mind the existing plan, consider the process for making amendments or changes to that plan, and the likelihood of support or opposition to those changes from the other entities that rely on its property tax revenues. Creating and utilizing an urban renewal area is complex. It is recommended that the group seek further training and the advice of the city attorney to determine whether this option fits the city's redevelopment goals.

TAX ABATEMENT

New housing construction can also be spurred by tax abatements. Tax abatement should never be used in a TIF district because the taxes generated on the value added by new development through TIF are used to pay down the general obligation bonds or debt incurred to incentivize the development. It is recommended that the group reach out to a land-use expert through the Area 15 Regional Planning Commission or the resources from the Iowa League of Cities when creating a tax abatement program. The Iowa League of Cities provides more information on its website (https://iowaleague.org/resource/property-tax-abatements-and-exemptions/).

INCENTIVES FOR DEVELOPER, BUILDER, AND BUYER

The creation and implementation of home building, buying assistance, and rehab incentive programs should be done in conjunction with support from the City of Fairfield and in concordance with existing plans. A community can incentivize new housing construction by targeting the developer/builder and the home buyer. When encouraging building on existing infill lots, a common incentive that can help bridge the value gap between the cost to build and the purchase price is for a city or partnering nonprofit development group to purchase the lots from private holders and resell or donate the lot for construction. Selling the lot at a loss can help overcome the value gap and benefit both the developer/builder and the home buyer, and is a process called "land banking." The Center for Community Progress provides a good primer on this process (https://communityprogress.org/resources/land-banks/). Be wary, however, that the Iowa State Code may not permit all practices promoted by national organizations such as the Center for Community Progress.

Utility incentives can be accomplished by waiving the connection fee for newly constructed homes either on the front end with the builder or on the back end with the buyer. These types of incentives can also be coupled with incentives from the natural gas utility provider, Black Hills Energy, which has rebate programs available for certain energy efficiency improvements (https://www.blackhillsenergy.com/efficiency-and-savings/residential-rebates/iowa-residential-rebates).

Building and connection fees can increase the cost of new development and deter new construction in a community. The short-term financial gain of permit surcharges is offset by the long-term property-tax benefit to the community. Newton, Iowa, has an aggressive single-family home construction assistance program. Adopted in 2014, the program waives building permit fees and covers the utility and interest costs of improvements for up to 12 months or when the property is sold, whichever occurs first (https://newtongov.org/806/Housing-Initiative). This program, along with a home buyer cash incentive, replaced Newton's tax abatement program. The project development work group should reach out to Newton as a first step in researching non-tax incentives.

Down-payment assistance programs typically target first-time home buyers or income-qualified households to cover part or all of the down payment. These programs can significantly lower the monthly mortgage payment, especially if it includes private mortgage insurance (PMI) for buyers unable to cover a 20-percent down payment of the total cost of the property.

Lenders can suggest federal and state down-payment assistance programs to home buyers. Likewise, a buyer can combine a locally created down-payment assistance program with state and federal funds. However, not every lender is versed in the range of programs available. Communities should work with lenders to market these programs to potential home buyers.

- Federal Down Payment Assistance Programs: https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/single-family-housing-direct-home-loans/ia
- State Down Payment Assistance Programs: https://www.iowafinance.com/homeownership/down-payment-programs/

Wright County created its own down-payment assistance home-buyer incentive program through Wright County Economic Development. The program does not require income qualification, takes the form of a loan, and requires a dollar-for-dollar match by the homebuyer (https://www.wrightcounty.iowa.gov/departments/economic_development/housing.php).

Newton, Iowa, offers an incentive package for which home buyers of a newly constructed home within a designated tax abatement district can qualify (https://newtongov.org/806/Housing-Initiative). This program is an example of how local communities can create more targeted guidelines to incentivize particular kinds of single-family dwellings. However, the more targeted a program, the fewer individuals will likely qualify or apply. Communities must consider a careful balance of income levels, new construction versus older homes, and geographic specificity.

Finally, there are local, state, and federal grants and loan opportunities available from government and non-government sources. Working with a local housing development corporation or the Area 15 Regional Planning Commission can increase municipal capacity to secure funding opportunities. The national nonprofit Housing Assistance Council is another resource for rural housing loans and technical assistance (https://ruralhome.org/).

RENTAL (LOW-INCOME) HOUSING

The Iowa Finance Authority provides information about funding sources that are directed toward developers of rental housing for individuals and families with low income, such as the federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program (https://www.iowafinance.com/programs-for-property-developers/housing-tax-credit-program/). The RHRA fact sheet on low-income housing tax credits provides entry-level background information on these processes (Appendix E, pp. 171–172).

MARKETING PLANS

Prior to rolling out grant and incentive programs, a marketing plan should be in place. Planning marketing strategies should begin when there is debate on what incentives and grants to create, continue through their creation, and be an integral part of their ongoing promotion. City staff can draw on local businesses and manufacturers to help craft the message and develop a communication plan. For instance, in Perry, the Dallas County Hospital plays an integral role in helping organizations develop promotional materials, while the Chamber of Commerce is crucial in promoting programs to businesses, the workforce, and community members. In addition, area newspapers and local radio stations still reach a large audience.

One of the best strategies to encourage housing development is to make entry into your community as simple as possible for potential developers. Creating a development packet to distribute to potential home builders that includes information about tax abatement and any applications or forms that need to be submitted for local incentives is a good idea. The resources or incentives highlighted in the packet should be dictated by the type of housing the city aims to attract.

Different developers focus on different types of housing. Some developers only construct senior housing; some may only do affordable housing projects using the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program. Other developers only build market-rate, multi-family housing. Some developers will only do new construction, while others are willing to consider adaptive reuse of larger, unused buildings such as old schools or warehouses. To target your promotional efforts, utilize the State of Iowa's database of registered contractors and developers to identify builders in your area (https://contractor.iowa.gov/IowaIWD/CREG/publicSearch/publicSearch.jsp).

An excellent place to connect with developers in Iowa is at the annual HousingIowa Conference sponsored by the Iowa Finance Authority (https://www.iowafinance.com/housingiowa-conference/). Developers with experience in tax-credit financing, Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) fund administration and distribution, and other types of funding often attend this conference because the Iowa Finance Authority provides a workshop on upcoming grants for developers. Community leaders can network with developers and learn from other communities working on similar projects across the state.

As a community, recruiting a developer also means identifying a location for new housing, working with the developer to acquire the property, working with the developer on site preparations such as utility extensions, assisting with applications and loan documents, and helping fill financial gaps.

RELEVANT TECHNICAL ASSISTANCE OPPORTUNITIES

The Community Visioning Program is an effort between Iowa State University, Trees Forever, and a private-sector landscape architect that integrates landscape planning and design with sustainable community action to assist cities in making meaningful decisions about the local landscape (http://www.communityvisioning.org). Sponsored by the Iowa Department of Transportation, the program empowers local leaders through a planning process that results in a transportation enhancement plan reflecting the values and identity of the community. Evaluation research has shown that 98% of participating communities complete at least one project and nearly 50 percent complete four or more projects. Types of projects developed through Community Visioning include streetscape improvements, community branding and signage, park improvements along major roadways, and trail development and enhancement. Communities selected for the program must commit $2,000 toward project implementation. Applications are due in September. Funding resources for implementing these types of projects can be found in Trees Forever's online Community Project Funding Guide (https://treesforever.org/Community-Project-Funding-Guide/). Fairfield has already benefited by participating in Community Visioning in 2003 and again in 2015, but remains eligible to apply again.

CLEANUP EVENTS

Community cleanup days are popular ways to entice households to get rid of difficult items to dispose of because of size, bulk, or toxic material. Cities can facilitate this process by collecting appliances, tires, mattresses, paints and chemicals, and brush and/or construction materials once a year, hauling it to the landfill, and paying the tipping fee. Most cities "staff" the cleanups to make sure that only appropriate items are being collected and only households are being served. Some cities work with volunteer groups to help senior citizens and disabled individuals get some of these larger items off their properties or out of their basements.

BUILDING CODE ENFORCEMENT

One issue that surfaced during the RHRA workbook exercise is that the city's staff has had inconsistent access to professional development opportunities for code enforcement and building inspection. As part of the evaluation of staff capacity, the city will need to decide how to divide the different duties of code enforcement and nuisance abatement. Many communities in Iowa do not have a designated employee whose sole job is to do this task; however, given that code enforcement has arisen as an area of chronic concern, the city should consider hiring a full-time code enforcement clerk.

Here are some things to consider should the city desire to hire a code enforcement clerk/inspector/officer:

1. Consider what skills or certifications the code enforcement clerk/inspector/officer will need to guarantee that they have adequate knowledge to accomplish tasks related to existing housing, remodels, inspection of new construction, nuisance abatement, and commercial structures.
2. Consider how the code enforcement clerk/inspector/officer will interact with zoning officials, the city council, and the police department to ensure compliance with property owners.
3. Consider long-term financing for this position by "loaning" the clerk/inspector/officer to neighboring communities for a set fee to offset some costs.
4. Build in time for professional development to assure that the code enforcement clerk/inspector/officer has a supportive network throughout the state and stays current on all Iowa building codes and laws.

Community code enforcement involves individual residents and community or neighborhood organizations in the identification of nuisance properties and other housing problems. The Center for Community Progress provides three case studies of community code enforcement in larger metro areas (https://www.communityprogress.net/tool-3--community-code-enforcement-partnerships-pages-269.php).

The law firm of Jones & Mayer describes the impetus and mechanisms for proactive, community member-driven code enforcement. These approaches could be tailored for your community needs (https://www.jones-mayer.com/news/2020/11/10/reactive-v-proactive-code-enforcement/).

State code regulating nuisance abatement can be found in Iowa Code Chapter 657 (https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/code/657.pdf).

Finally, crowdsourcing information and community involvement in reporting code violations or hazardous areas is an excellent way to engage with residents in improving their neighborhoods.

POCKET NEIGHBORHOOD

Creating a pocket neighborhood can appear to be an ambitious project; however, the main steps fall into the broad categories of identifying locations, including lots and owners; acquiring the lots; assembling the sites, including demolishing existing structures; acquiring funding and/or investors; and opening a call for developers/builders, including determining if additional incentives are needed to attract builders. This is a complex activity in and of itself and will likely require bringing on additional partners such as the council of governments for assistance with grant applications, or potentially private construction or planning services.

This working group would benefit from talking through the process with others in Iowa who have accomplished creating new housing. One example is Maquoketa, which was mentioned during the RHRA educational workshop (Appendix B, pp. 49–50). Maquoketa Bear River Cottages is a small pocket neighborhood developed in partnership with East Central Development Corporation (ECDC) and the East Central Intergovernmental Association (ECIA). More information is available on ECIA's website (https://ecia.org/pocketneighborhood/).

Another project that may prove instructive is the Birdland Area, a flood reclamation and recovery project that partnered with Habitat for Humanity of Greater Des Moines to build affordable, owner-occupied homes. This kind of partnership could be a model, whether it be a local chapter of Habitat for Humanity, partnering with a community college or high school building-trades program (possibly linked with the carpentry program at Iowa Central Community College in Fort Dodge), working with an organization such as Homes for Iowa, or a combination of affordable building options. Combining the new construction with a home buyer's assistance program such as the USDA 502 program could significantly lower the cost of entry for home buyers. Information about the Birdland Area Redevelopment Plan is available online (https://www.msa-ps.com/our-experience/birdland-area-redevelopment-plan-des-moines-iowa/).

A second project in Des Moines that could be instructive is Washington Court in the Riverbend neighborhood, which details the complexity of accumulating small lots from multiple owners in a low-resource neighborhood (https://www.housingiowaconference.com/media/cms/Washington_Court_Development__Web_FE112AB15B261.pdf).

Finally, the Grinnell Garden Cottages neighborhood in Grinnell demonstrates that new construction priced in the mid-range can be affordable for middle-income qualified households (starting at approximately $50,000 yearly household income) with the right incentive structure. Grinnell Garden Cottages is also an excellent example of the range of partners required to make the development happen (https://grinnellgardencottages.com/).

INFILL DEVELOPMENT

The blog post "Infill Development Supports Community Connectivity" on the American Planning Association website presents the many ways utilizing infill developments can benefit a community, and lists steps and strategies for this type of development (https://www.planning.org/blog/9227414/infill-development-supports-community-connectivity/). Although many of the examples used are in larger metropolitan areas, the concepts are applicable to smaller communities.
