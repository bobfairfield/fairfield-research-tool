COMMUNITY VISION OVERVIEW
Fairfield Forever is a comprehensive plan for the City of Fairfield to help guide growth and decision-making through the year 2040. The Fairfield Forever
plan was given this name because it is meant to reflect a shared vision for the future of the community. A comprehensive plan allows a community to
evaluate its strengths, weaknesses and opportunities for positive change. A community vision is identified through an analysis of the existing conditions
and through public participation. This chapter will explain the planning process, the plan’s major components, and then overview the community vision
that helped inform the goals, policies and action items recommended within the plan.

COMMUNITY VISION

PURPOSE OF THE PLAN
Comprehensive plans are guiding documents for growth and development
in a community. They act as information management tools that create an
inventory of existing conditions and set goals and recommendations for
the future. The comprehensive planning process provides an opportunity
for residents to directly engage in goal setting for their community.
These engagement opportunities help a city to define and build upon the
community’s character. The analysis and public engagement should help
the city to establish an implementation plan that provides a roadmap for
accomplishing the strategies identified in the plan. Lastly, a comprehensive
plan should not be viewed as a static document. The plan, especially the
goals, policies and action items, should be reviewed regularly to assess
how things may have changed and to ensure that the community vision is
being implemented. The goals, policy statements and action items should
be reviewed annually and the entire document reviewed every five years.

Plan Horizon Year & Boundary
The Fairfield Forever plan has a horizon year of 2040. This roughly twentyyear timeframe will include short-, mid-, and long-term goals and policies
for the community. All projections for growth and facility expansion will be
based on likely need in the year 2040. While this plan is meant to forecast
through the year 2040, routine review of the document is an important step
to make sure the plan is still relevant and that tasks are being accomplished.
A geographic planning boundary was created for the future land use plan.
The extents of this boundary can be described roughly as:
• West to Hemlock Avenue from 200th Street to Cedar Creek
• North to 185th Street from Kelp Avenue to Salina Road
• East to the creek just past Osage Avenue from 200th Street to
• South to approximately 1 mile south of the Highway 34 & 1 interchange
Not all areas of the planning boundary will be developed during the planning
horizon year. However, it is important to identify a strategy for these areas to
reference as new plans are drafted and considered by the city. Due to its size,
much of the planning boundary will likely remain agricultural, but protecting
this important resource is also in the interest of the Fairfield community.

Planning Process
The Fairfield Forever planning process was divided over four phases in an
approximately one-year period. The four phases were divided as followed:
Phase 1 - Kick-off, Research & Analysis
Phase 1 involved a kick-off meeting with the steering committee, data
collection and the analysis of existing conditions within Fairfield.
Phase 2 - Vision, Input & Direction
Phase 2 covered most of the public engagement for the plan. During
this phase, there was a public workshop, community survey, stakeholder
interviews, a steering committee meeting, a city council workshop and a
kid-focused box city event.
Phase 3 - Draft Plan & Evaluation
Phase 3 included the actual writing of the full draft plan and a steering
committee meeting.
Phase 4 - Final Draft Plan
Phase 4 included the finalizing of the draft and the public approval process
through the Planning & Zoning Commission and the City Council.

Plan Components
The plan components are as follows:
• Community Vision
• Community Profile
• Public Participation
• Natural Resources
• Parks & Recreation
• Public Facilities
• Infrastructure
• Community Character
• Land Use
• Housing
• Transportation & Mobility
• Implementation

Most of the chapters will also include a list of goals, policies and action items
related to each chapter subject. Goals are objectives or aims which may be
broad or specific. Policies represent on-going principles by which the City
should adhere when approving new development or planning future public
infrastructure investments. Action Items are specific steps and activities the
City should take.

Past Plans
To prepare for the Fairfield Forever plan, previous planning efforts completed
by the City were reviewed. This includes the 1995 Comprehensive Plan, the
2012 Community-Wide Strategic Plan and the Go Green Strategic Plan.

COMMUNITY VISION OVERVIEW
Throughout the planning process, there were common themes heard in
all discussions and public input methods. The themes that appeared to be
common amongst most community members revolve around the following
ideas: housing, jobs and sustainability.

Housing
Housing was the final key theme persistent throughout the public
engagement and analysis completed for the Fairfield Forever comprehensive
plan. Fairfield’s housing has character and charm, but it also presents as
an obstacle to growth. . New housing, both in the form of owner-occupied
single-family homes at all price levels and quality multi-family dwellings units,
will be necessary to grow the community. The community’s existing singlefamily housing stock and its great older neighborhoods are further in need
of rejuvenation as these homes can further provide much needed housing.
There is a desire to help rehabilitate homes and neighborhoods throughout
Fairfield to help have the health and wellness that the town is known for be
reflected in the quality and vibrancy of the community’s housing. Identifying
solutions to rehabilitating existing homes as well as areas to build new
housing was an overarching goal of this plan.

Jobs
Continuing to attract and retain not only jobs but qualified workers for the jobs
available in Fairfield is a main obstacle and opportunity for Fairfield. People
were clear in their basic desire to have a decent job that can help support
themselves and their families. There is a strong entrepreneurial spirit in this
town in addition to a healthy manufacturing industry. A goal of this plan will
be to identify strategies and areas for employment growth within Fairfield for
the future. Skilled workers from the building trades (plumbers, electricians,
finish carpenters) are in great demand to both remodel and renovate the
existing housing stock and to help fuel the steady growth of the community

Sustainability
There is a strong desire within the Fairfield community to maintain and
cultivate a culture that is ingrained with sustainable practices. This appears to
be for all three lenses of sustainability: environmental, economic and equity.
The idea of sustainability is further linked with the idea of overall healthy
living and wellness in Fairfield. Fairfield is a community that is unafraid to
be bold in their efforts to grow sustainably. This is further reinforced by past
planning efforts including the Community-Wide Strategic Plan completed in
2012 and the Go-Green Strategic Plan 2020 that was completed in 2009 –
both successfully implemented over the past few decades.

ROLE OF THE CITY
The role of the City is to help facilitate the vision, recommendations and
priorities listed throughout this plan. Implementation of the plan will require
collaboration between the public and the private sector with support and
assistance from the residents of Fairfield. Specific policies and action
items for the City have been identified to help achieve the vision outlined
in Fairfield Forever. These goals and policies should be referenced when
making land use, funding and strategic planning decisions.

COMMUNITY PROFILE

FUTURE GROWTH
Population projections are an important component of any comprehensive
plan. It is critical to evaluate what the potential population of a community
will be in the future in order to adequately plan for it. In general, population
projections are based on recent growth trends and the likelihood that they
will continue. A series of projections have been completed for the Fairfield
Forever plan to provide a range of possible scenarios. An average of these
scenarios was then calculated to identify a balanced projection.
The population estimates between 2000-2017 have shown that the
population of Fairfield decreased between 2000-2010 after which the
community began to grow again. While the last seven years indicates a
positive growth trend in Fairfield, the population projections should consider
all possible scenarios including the potential for a population change similar
to the slight decline experienced between 2000-2010.
The six main projections used included:
• 1990-2017 average annual rate 0.2%
• 2000-2017 average annual rate 0.5%
• 2000-2010 average annual rate -0.1%
• 2010-2017 average annual rate 1.5%
• 2010-2015 average annual rate 1.4%
• Hamilton-Perry Projection
Based on recent population estimates, multiple growth rates were applied
to represent the wide range of options given the recent variability of
population in Fairfield. Both positive and negative growth rates were
explored as possibilities to help even out the effect of any outliers within the
data. Hamilton-Perry is a simplified variant of the cohort survival population
projection that is better suited for sub-county level population projections.
This project methodology looks at changes in population by age cohorts
and mimics a birth rate which is used to project future population at ten-year
increments.
The overall set of population projections indicate a positive growth rate for
Fairfield, assuming the factors that contributed to the population reversal

hold throughout the next twenty years. The 2020 average population
projection is 10,331 and the 2030 the projection is 11,076. The projections for
2040 range from 9,791 to 14,104. The average 2040 population projection is
11,924. Many factors will determine the actual outcome of population by 2040.
Based on the recent variation in population estimates, it is recommended
to evaluate the projections every five years to incorporate new population
estimates, especially the 2020 Decennial Census results.

COMMUNITY PROFILE

EXISTING LAND USE
The land use pattern in Fairfield has developed organically since the town’s
inception over one-hundred and fifty years go. There is an old downtown
that serves as the main cultural and civic hub of the community. Two major
transportation routes, Burlington Avenue and Highway 1 / Main Street, divide
the community into four main quadrants.

Lower Density Residential
Like many small towns, the dominant land use in Fairfield today is lower
density residential. There are major pockets of lower density residential
land in the areas south, east and northeast of the downtown. There are
several other areas of lower density residential scattered throughout the
community, often adjacent to what is often considered incompatible land
uses such as industrial land.

Figure 2.18 shows the percentage of owner and renter-occupied housing.
While some of the rental housing is composed of apartments, townhomes
or other higher density housing types, often the rental housing appears to
be converted single-family homes. This occupancy pattern has an effect on
neighborhoods and the overall quality of the housing available in Fairfield.
These five factors play a large role in the character and signatures of Fairfield
while also contributing to the community’s strengths and weaknesses now
and looking forward.

Industrial
Industrial land is another large land use that defines Fairfield. Much of the
industrial land is located along the remaining railway line that bisects the
community north of the downtown. There is also industrial land that sits along
a remnant railway corridor that runs north-south west of the downtown.

Parks & Open Space
Fairfield is fortunate to have a large amount of park, recreation and open
space throughout the community. In addition to a robust park system, there
is a large amount of tree cover and several larger lakes located in the
community offering additional recreation and environmental benefits.

Public, Semi-Public & Institutional Land
Fairfield is the county seat for Jefferson County and is home to the Maharishi
University of Management. These two factors impact the land use pattern by
creating a high amount of public, semi-public or institutional land. This has
an impact on the tax base as well as the land use pattern.

Rental Housing
The presence of a university in the community, is one factor that contributes
to the above average amount of rental housing units located in Fairfield.

Fairfield is an anomaly in rural Iowa in that it has experienced net population
growth in recent years while other communities have struggled to maintain
even their existing population. Housing availability can be a major factor
in the ability of a community to attract population and employers. The
housing available in Fairfield is predominantly single-family homes. Of the
roughly 4,300 housing units in Fairfield, 60% are owner-occupied. The
median owner-occupied home value in Fairfield is $104,100 according to
U.S. Census 2017 estimates. The median assessed value for single-family
homes in Fairfield according to Jefferson County tax assessor is $101,600.
Generally, housing in Fairfield is lower valued and this can have impacts in
attracting new residents who may be looking for higher value homes.

INFRASTRUCTURE OVERVIEW
A detailed analysis of the current public infrastructure within Fairfield and its planning area was not performed as part of this comprehensive plan.
However, contained within this chapter is an overview of the current infrastructure system along with recommendations for further study area and
opportunities that should be explored in order to address the vision and goals developed as part of this planning process.

INFRASTRUCTURE

CURRENT MUNICIPAL INFRASTRUCTURE
The City provides water and sanitary sewer services to most of its businesses
and residents and is further responsible for maintaining and improving a
public street network and storm water system. The City currently has a multiyear phased sanitary sewer improvement plan (see diagram). Considering
the community’s steady growth rate, the level of service for these public
infrastructure items and the ability to expand to new growth areas should
continually be monitored to ensure opportunities for new construction are
not hindered by a lack of services or infrastructure capacity.
The Future Land Use Map identifies commercial and industrial growth areas
along the Highway 34 corridor at both the east and west edges of Fairfield
and at Hwy 1. Additional areas for residential growth have been identified
along the northern end of the City. In general, these areas are well served
with existing public streets. Additional transportation related comments and
recommendations are identified in Chapter 11, Transportation & Mobility.
However, the timely delivery of water and sanitary sewer service is critical
to attract new development. Therefore, the City must continue to plan and
budget for sanitary sewer and water infrastructure expansions.
The City owns and maintains approximately 91 miles of water main and 65.5
miles of sanitary sewer and 61 miles of city streets. Miles of stormwater
is unknown since much of the system is unmapped. Much of the city’s
infrastructure is over 50 years old. A comprehensive replacement plan is
necessary to continue to provide these services to the existing population.

Over 70% of the sanitary sewer pipe is made of clay and will require
significant maintenance in the years to come. There are twelve (12) pump
stations, eight (8) with minor grinder pumps. The two major pump stations
within the public collection system is Grease Lightening Lift Station and
Highway 34 Lift Station.
With the completion of STEP 2 sanitary sewer improvements, expansion of
the east side of the community can easily be served with gravity sanitary
sewer. Expansion to the north and west will likely require additional pump
stations, which should be considered when developing.
Upon completion of the wastewater plant improvements, the plan will have a
treatment capacity of 9 million gallons per day (MGD), allowing for significant
growth.

WATER SYSTEM CONSIDERATIONS
The water treatment plant has a capacity of 4 million gallons per day (MGD),
allowing for growth.
There are two water towers in town:
• B Street (500,000 gallons)
• Grimes (750,000 gallons)
As growth expands, a new water tower(s) may be needed. The City has
preliminarily identified the southwest corner of the wastewater treatment
plant site as a potential site for a southern tower.

INFRASTRUCTURE

OPPORTUNITIES FOR NEW SERVICES
Through years of hard work and investment on the part of numerous
property owners with the City of Fairfield, the community has amassed a
significant amount of local electrical power generation in the form of onsite photovoltaic (PV) systems. These systems are very visible and make
apparent to any visitor that Fairfield takes sustainable power generation
seriously and is a leader in renewable power generation at the local level.
Not only does this support sustainability goals of the City but serves as
an important economic development driver. Many businesses, as well as
residents, are beginning to include the availability of renewable energy as
part their decision-making process for where they wish to locate.

ensuring that electrical service remain reliable and that pricing remain stable.
However, even considering the obstacles, becoming 100% supported by
renewalable energy is a major goal of the City and further study should be
considered.

This significant volume of locally generated, renewal electric power also
provides Fairfield with a unique opportunity. At some point in time it may
be financially practical for the community to become its own local electrical
utility provider due to the continued loss in revenues generated from the
Fairfield territory likely being experienced by the current franchise electrical
utility. As more property owners generate their own electrical power, they
will need to purchase less from the current franchise, continuing the revenue
decline. For the City to take over as the electrical power provider, it may
need to purchase or buy-out the current franchise’s local electrical power
distribution system. This system would likely be valued based on its ability
to generate revenue. As revenues decline, it should become less expensive
to purchase and the seller should be more motivated to sell.
With the City becoming the local electrical provider, the entire community
will have the opportunity to further its vision for being supported by 100%
renewable-only energy. As its owner electrical utility, Fairfield will be able
to make decisions on purchasing electrical power from renewal generation
sources or even constructing its own utility scale wind generation farms or
solar generation systems.
It should be acknowledged that doing so is not a simple process and may
take numerous years of study, analysis, negotiation, and construction. A
significant and worthy concern that will need to be fully addressed is

1. Maintain the City’s infrastructure to support the health and growth of the community
P1 – Continue to plan and budget for necessary infrastructure maintenance and expansion
P2 – Consider incorporation of energy efficiency and long lifecycle construction methods and technologies as part of infrastructure replacement and
improvements

2. Be prepared to protect and recover from natural disasters

factors, in a community. Land use planning helps dictate where people can live, work and spend their free time. One major component of the Fairfield
Forever plan is the creation of a new future land use plan map. The future land use map shows the ideal development or redevelopment pattern of

applications and new development proposals. This chapter reviews the existing land use conditions in Fairfield and discusses the factors that went into
the final future land use plan.

FUTURE LAND USE CATEGORIES
Agricultural

The Agricultural land use category is land that should remain set aside for
agricultural land or similar low intensity uses. Local food production is an
important industry for Fairfield and is a major factor in the character and
quality of life in the community. Unnecessary conversion of agricultural land
to developed land should be avoided if possible, to protect this important
local, state and national resource. Residential development should be
limited to no more than one dwelling unit per 40 acres.

LAND USE PLAN

FUTURE LAND USE CATEGORIES

FUTURE LAND USE CATEGORIES

The Rural Residential land use category includes large lot single-family
homes located in the more agricultural and natural areas of Fairfield, outside
the current city limits. These lots may or may not be part of an existing
agricultural enterprise and are typically very low density at 1 dwelling unit
per 5 acres. The Rural Residential category can also provide land suitable
for sustainable-driven, non-traditional housing developments such as ecovillages and cluster subdivisions.

The Forest Reserve land use category includes all those areas within the
planning boundary that are designated Forest Reserves in Jefferson County.
These areas are generally tree-dense and adjacent to open space, parks or
other lower density development types. Tree cover is an important natural
resource and contributes to the quality of life in Fairfield. These areas should
remain undeveloped and preserved as forested land.

FUTURE LAND USE CATEGORIES

Open Space & Stream Corridor are those areas within the 100-year
floodplain and within a 100-foot buffer along Type 1 - Perennial Streams
that are not already set aside as park land or forest reserve. These areas
are environmentally sensitive and should be protected from most types of
development to protect the ecological function of the land and to avoid
property damage locally and downstream from increased runoff if the site
were to be developed.

The Low-Density Residential land use category includes traditional singlefamily dwelling units with lot sizes ranging from 1 to 5 dwelling units per acre.
This district also includes small lot and “tiny” homes and triplexes to try and
increase flexibility and affordability within the housing stock in Fairfield. This
area will predominantly be made up of owner-occupied single-family homes
with some duplexes, triplexes, and rental housing unit types. Development
in this area may also include cluster subdivisions, religious, educational,
institutional uses, and public and private recreational areas.

The Higher Density Residential category is for areas that will include a
mixture of low, medium and higher density housing. For example, this land
use category could have single-family homes adjacent to townhomes and
apartment buildings. Densities will range from 5 to 12 dwelling units per acre
for townhomes and 12+ dwelling units per acre for apartment or condominiums.
This land use may also include religious, educational, institutional, and public
and private parks and structures. This land use can act as a buffer between
Low-Density Residential and more intense land uses.

The Neighborhood Commercial category includes small retail and office
uses intended to serve the daily retail needs of residents. Typical land uses
include daycares, assisted living facilities, small office, convenience stores
and other neighborhood-serving uses. The site and building design within
this category should be of a suitable scale for the adjacent neighborhood
and should not cause unnecessary interruption to the nearby residential
areas through noise, light or vehicular traffic. Sites are generally 1 to 10 acres
and should accommodate pedestrian and bicycle circulation.

FUTURE LAND USE CATEGORIES

Burlington Avenue Commercial is a special land use category for the highwayfacing lots along Burlington Avenue west of downtown Fairfield. This corridor
is an important entry into downtown Fairfield from the Burlington Avenue
interchange on Highway 34. Commercial development in this area should
be complementary to the downtown entertainment and retail environment.
These uses should be accessible for bikes and pedestrians and should
feature a limited number of curb cuts and entrances. Uses should also be
neighborhood friendly due to their proximity to residential dwellings.

The Community Commercial land use category is designed for communityserving retail, entertainment and commercial areas along the western half
of Burlington Avenue near the Highway 34 interchange. These retail and
entertainment uses are intended to serve the entire Fairfield community
as well as attract users from outside the city limits. These locations should
contain multiple access points and consist of larger box stores and multitenant shopping centers.

The Highway Commercial land use category is designed for commercial
activity that may occur near major highways with the intent of providing
commercial products and services to both local residents and the traveling
public. This land has been strategically placed along the major interchanges
in Fairfield along Highway 34. Typical uses include gas stations, car washes,
car dealerships, fast food and sit-down restaurants, convenience stores,
hotels, banks and auto repair stores.

The Downtown Core District is the area immediately adjacent to the Central
Park downtown square and also includes the Fairfield Arts & Convention
Center and the Jefferson County Courthouse. This downtown core should
be exclusively first-floor retail or restaurant with limited amount of other
commercial or office uses to encourage all-day activity. The second floor
should be residential. Facade and streetscape improvements should be
focused on this core area to enhance the pedestrian experience.

The Entrepreneurial Area is a land use category dedicated to industrial
areas that are scattered among residential areas. This area will include
a mixture of residential, commercial, and industrial uses. The category is
meant to encourage entrepreneurism by having less rigid design standards
and separation requirements between different land uses. Buildings that
combine living and working (live-work units) are encouraged to support
creativity, entrepreneurism and self-employment.

The Office land use category is reserved for areas designed to retain and
promote employment opportunities in Fairfield. This land category is meant
for a combination of professional office, medical, research and development,
and single or multi-tenant buildings that are one (1) or more stories tall. This
land use category may include some retail activity to support the offices such
as coffee shops, restaurants and dry cleaners. This land use category can
serve as a buffer between residential areas and more intense commercial
and industrial uses.

The Industrial Business Park category is for light industrial uses as well as
warehouse and distribution facilities. Appropriate light industrial activity
includes fabrication, manufacturing, assembly or processing of materials that
are in refined form and do not require transformation that creates smoke,
gas, odor, dust, vibrations, soot, or other noxious elements. Lot size will vary
but will typically require larger lots with a minimum of 10,000 square feet.

The Industrial land use category is set aside for large-scale assembly of
goods and materials processing and may include outdoor storage. Uses
may also include those uses allowed within the Office and Industrial Business
Park categories. This land is for industrial uses considered more intense
than those listed in the Industrial Business Park and therefore should be
located away from major residential areas which are generally incompatible
with industrial sites.

PUBLIC FACILITIES OVERVIEW
Public facilities are those buildings, organizations and programs that provide essential services to the residents of Fairfield. Some of these facilities are
related to public safety such as police, fire and emergency medical services. Others improve the overall quality of life for residents such as libraries and
community events. Other important services include schools, hospitals and government services. This chapter provides an overview of the existing public

Tiny Homes and Accessory Dwelling Units
Interest has been growing nationally in a small, more affordable, singlefamily housing type referred to as “Tiny Homes.” Tiny homes are detached,
single-family structures generally less than 500 square feet in size - often
resembling a small cabin. These types of homes are typically owner
occupied and located on their own lot but sometimes share a lot with a
larger, traditional single-family home and serve as a second dwelling unit.
Tiny homes can be congregated as part of a small lot subdivision (lots that
are less than 50 feet in width) or built upon a small infill lot within a traditional
single-family development.
Second dwelling units are typically referred to as Accessory Dwelling Units
(ADUs). An ADU is a separate and smaller second dwelling that is located
within the lot or building envelop of a single-family dwelling and is held under
the same ownership of that single-family dwelling. This separate dwelling
contains its own living, cooking, and housekeeping facilities, may or may not
have its own entrance, and is contained within or attached to the principal
single-family dwelling. Examples include: a dwelling over an attached or
detached garage, a dwelling within the basement of the principal building,
or a dwelling attached to the principal building.
ADUs can help fill a community’s need for affordable housing and provide
income to first-time home buyers to offset some of their home mortgage
payment. These second dwelling units can further be an option for families
to live in close proximity to their parents and grandparents, helping them
remain independent longer. Some communities require that the owner of
the property must remain as a permanent occupant of either dwelling unit
at all times.

NATURAL RESOURCES
The natural environment is strongly tied to the future growth and development of Fairfield. As a community with ample access to nature through parks,
lakes, streams and open space, developing a strategy to protect and preserve the environment is critical. This chapter divides environmental conditions
into two main categories: hydrology and land cover.

NATURAL RESOURCES

HYDROLOGY
Hydrology refers to where water comes from, where it travels to and where it may go. While nearly every environmental feature can trace back to water, the
specific areas evaluated for Fairfield include:
• Streams & Lakes
• Watershed
• Wetlands
• Floodplain

Streams & Lakes

Floodplain

Streams and lakes play an important role in the environmental health of
an area by conveying water, organisms, and nutrients. They help store and
drain water and provide habitat for plants and animals. Streams and lakes
provide the following economic and environmental benefits:
• Filter pollutants
• Supply drinking water
• Fish and wildlife habitat
• Irrigate crops
• Mitigate damage from floods • Recreation and commercial activities

Floodplains is generally located in low lying areas along streams, tributaries
and other bodies of water that conveys water that has risen above the stream
bank. The floodplain can be the first line of defense or the first casualty of
property damage and other destruction in high water events. Ideally, the
floodplain is left mainly undeveloped and foliage dense so that when the
floodplain is inundated with water, the permeable ground helps slow and
absorb water to prevent property damage and naturally filter contaminants
out of the water. Proper floodplain management helps protect water quality
degradation and provides a biologically rich habitat for flora and fauna.

There are many ways to classify stream types, but one common way is by
stream type. Type 1 streams are known as perennial streams that typically
have water flowing through them year-round. Type 2 streams are known
as intermittent and only flow during certain times of the year and may stop
flowing during dry periods. Type 1 and 2 streams can best be protected
by creating a protective buffer corridor around them. The Environmental
Protective Agency suggests a 100-foot buffer around type 1 streams and a
50-foot buffer around type 2 streams.
Fairfield has four type 1 streams within the city limits all which flow into Cedar
Creek within 3 miles of the city limits:
• Crow Creek / Troy Creek
• Kankakee Creek
• West, center and east branches of Indian Creek
• East branch of Mitchell Creek

The formal floodplain boundaries are identified through the Federal
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), which is part of the Department
of Homeland Security. The two main designations are 100 and 500-year
floodplain. The 100-year floodplain are those areas with a 0.1% probability
of occurring in a given year. The 500-year floodplain are those areas with
a 0.2% probability of occurring in a given year. Both floodplain types are
shown in Map 2.
In general, all new development should be prohibited within the official
100-year and discouraged in the 500-year floodplain. Fairfield’s Code of
Ordinances currently establishes several floodplain related districts that only
permit a certain limited amount of uses.

The City of Fairfield owns all three of the larger lakes in Fairfield, providing
an excellent opportunity for watershed buffers. They include:
• Bonnifield Lake
• Pleasant Lake
• Walton Lake

NATURAL RESOURCES

HYDROLOGY
Wetlands
Wetlands can be simply defined areas of land that are saturated with water
at least part of the year. Wetlands may only be saturated during specific
seasons or during periods of heavy rain fall or snow melt. They are usually
found along streams, lakes and in the floodplain. Wetlands play an important
role in the ecological health of an area by:
• Filter water
• Provide flood and erosion control
• Fish and wildlife habitat
• Property damage prevention
• Recreation activities
The type of wetlands located within Fairfield include riverine (rivers),
lacustrine (lake), and palustrine (marsh, forested).

Watersheds
Watersheds can be broadly defined as the “…land area that channels
rainfall and snowmelt to creeks, streams, and rivers, and eventually to
outflow points such as reservoirs, bays, and the ocean. There are multiple
scales of watersheds that are outlined as Hydrologic Unit Codes (HUCs)
that range from very large to very small. Watersheds outline the path and
interconnectedness of water systems within an area. The subwatershed
boundaries for Fairfield are shown in Map 3. Fairfield is mainly divided
between three subwatersheds: Crow Creek, Big Branch- Cedar Creek, and
Church Creek - Cedar Creek.
Watershed management can best be managed through proper land use
and stormwater management. Impermeable surfaces, such as concrete,
roads and development, alter the natural drainage patterns of an area
and can lead to excessive volumes of water entering the system as runoff
instead of permeating into the ground as would occur naturally. Watershed
management impacts all areas of hydrology within an area and is a prime
concern for environmental and land use planning.

NATURAL RESOURCES

LAND COVER
The physical geography of the land plays an important role in where
development should occur, what areas are worth special protection, and
where growth should be promoted within a community. To understand these
features in Fairfield, the following have been reviewed:
• Tree Cover
• Agricultural Land
• Slope
• Soils

Tree Cover
Trees are a precious environmental resource that while technically
renewable, takes decades to replace. There are documented ecological
and economic benefits to tree cover in a community including:
• Reduced urban heat island effect
• Carbon capture
• Habitat for bird and other wildlife
• Lower localized temperatures
• Increased property values and quality of life
The tree cover of Fairfield is shown in Map 5. Fairfield has a significant
amount of natural tree cover compared to other locations in Iowa. Fairfield
has two forested park areas in Lamson Wood and Witham Woods. In addition
to these forest parks, a significant amount of tree cover in Fairfield and
the greater Jefferson County area is designated as forest reserve through
Jefferson County. Healthy tree cover can be maintained in a community by
adopting practices such as requiring a diverse number of trees species (tree
disease protection) and replacing two trees for every one tree lost.

Agricultural Land
Agricultural land makes up a large amount of the area surrounding Fairfield.

BEST MANAGEMENT PRACTICES
Fairfield has natural resources that are essential for the culture and identity
of the community. As Fairfield grows, it will be integral to preserve the natural
features of the community. As the city works towards maximizing growth
and development opportunities of the community, it is essential to minimize
the negative environmental impacts of development. There are several
best management practices related to protecting the natural resources that
should be part of any strategy for long-term growth in the community.

Green Infrastructure
Green infrastructure is an approach to water management that protects,
restores, or mimics the natural water cycle. There are a variety of applications
including the planting of trees, restoration of wetlands, water efficiency
measures and floodplain protection. The extent of green infrastructure
ranges from site specific to regional. Fairfield should integrate the use of
green infrastructure practices in infrastructure and development in the forms
of rain gardens, bio-swales, and permeable pavements.

Green Streets
The purpose of green streets is for natural infiltration by using vegetation
and other pavement materials, such as crushed stone and pavers, which
minimize the commonly used impervious surfaces of concrete and asphalt.
Narrow streets, particularly in residential neighborhoods, allow space for
natural filtration to be implemented. Eliminating curbs or adding curb cuts
allow stormwater runoff to be directed into pervious areas and filtered
through low impact development features such as bioswales, permeable
pavers, or other green features.

Low-Impact Development (LID)
Stormwater management approach that seeks to manage runoff using
distributed and decentralized micro-scale controls. Infiltrate, filter, store
evaporate and detail runoff close to the source. The prevalence of slowdraining, clay-rich soils in the Fairfield area may make this approach difficult
in certain areas so understanding the soil conditions of a site is important.

Land-Use Development
Communities experience a large reduction in runoff if development is done
on poorly used properties in abandoned or underutilized commercial areas
opposed to development in greenfield sites. By directing the development in
target areas that were designed for the growth, the city can better manage
the stormwater runoff and reduce the total impervious area. Expanding
mixed-use development can be implemented in Fairfield to require less
pavement that protects water quality by reducing land consumption, parking
requirements, and vehicle use.

Non-Structural and Structural Stormwater Management
Non-structural stormwater practices include minimizing and/or disconnecting
of impervious surfaces by design development that reduces the runoff and
restores natural areas. The natural areas include riparian areas, wetlands and
forests, and vegetated areas that block roof and driveway runoff. Structural
stormwater practices typically requiring construction projects designed by
engineering plans that include ponds, wetlands, underground or surface
chambers or filters, bioretention areas and swales.

Stormwater Retrofitting
Stormwater retrofitting are a series of techniques to restore watersheds by
providing stormwater treatment in areas where practices did not previously
exist or were ineffective. They are generally installed at older, existing
stormwater facilities. The goal of stormwater retrofitting is to capture and
treat stormwater before it is delivered to the receiving waters.

Protecting Open and Natural Areas
Preserving existing natural areas is essential to reduce stormwater flooding
and sediment runoff. The areas with significant hydrologic functions, such as
forested areas or riparian corridors, will need to be protected. Implementing
and preserving other parks and open space to be used as buffer areas for
stormwater and to reduce flooding.

COMMUNITY CHARACTER
Community character are those features, places or cultural components that make a place special or different. Fairfield has cultivated a unique culture
for a rural community in southeast Iowa. This identity is largely due to its commitment to sustainability, diversity and the wide range of entertainment and
cultural opportunities available to residents because of the local university, historic downtown, and convention center. City actions can often maintain and
enhance the community character of a community and this chapter describes opportunities for Fairfield to help retain Fairfield’s brand.

COMMUNITY CHARACTER

CULTURE / UNIQUE VIBE
Fairfield has sustained its unique cultural community throughout decades
of both population growth and decline. In recent years, Fairfield has
outperformed similar sized cities with a population growth rate comparable
to larger Iowa cities. The growth and development have been built off core
values of diversity, sustainability, and well-being. The art and music culture
have produced a sense of community that is vibrant and unified. The residents
take pride in Fairfield’s dedication to cultivating a safe and integrated
community that celebrates diversity and health. There was substantial
positive feedback of how the parks and trails throughout the Fairfield region
have become an integral aspect of the community. Continuing the on-going
efforts by the Fairfield Visitors & Convention Bureau to market Fairfield’s
unique “vibe” will be an effective way to distinguish Fairfield from other
towns to attract people and businesses.

WAYFINDING & SIGNAGE
As Fairfield continues to experience growth, a well-developed and engaging
signage strategy should be implemented to create a sense of place and
community for all residents and visitors. A cohesive and consistent signage plan
can help visitors and residents feel more welcome and convey the message
that Fairfield is an inclusive community, open to all ages and backgrounds.
Signage in the gateways into the community as well as in the downtown
area are especially important. Additionally, signage can be incorporated in all
residential neighborhoods and commercial districts to unify the factions of a
city. Neighborhood-specific signage can be a valuable approach to ensuring
all residential neighborhoods feel part of the community.

Wayfinding
Wayfinding is a network of signage that gives information and direction to
visitors to orient themselves throughout the community. Proper wayfinding
signage highlights the primary characteristics of a community, such as
directions to the Sondheim Theater or the Central Park. It caters to visitors,
promotes a pedestrian-friendly community, and displays the vital aspects of
Fairfield. Wayfinding is a tool to enhance community identity in order to unify
the characteristics of Fairfield.

The Fairfield community has a thriving local arts and music culture, a key
feature highlighted during the public engagement process by residents.
Creating coordinated programs to install additional public art throughout
Fairfield is one approach to continue to encourage and incorporate
community expression. Showcasing local artist is an especially effective
way to highlight how Fairfield collaborates and creates cultural centers for
its residents. The rotating art collection in the Fairfield Arts & Convention
Center is a great example of showing local artists and art. Cinefest Fairfield
is another example of effective way to celebrate the cultural diversity of
Fairfield. Successfully marketing of and expanding up on these events will
help keep Fairfield a hot spot for art and culture throughout Iowa.

VOLUNTEERISM
One way to promote public involvement in Fairfield is making volunteerism
an integral aspect of the community. Not only does it benefit local nonprofit organizations and assists them in accomplishing their goals, but it
strengthens the relationships and union between residents by creating
a shared experience. Further, it can help foster understanding between
different groups of people by exposing them to difficulties some residents
may have. By distributing the responsibility of dealing with community
hardships among residents, deeper connections can be made between
residents and a great sense of belonging and investment in a community
can occur. Volunteering is an effective way to accomplish community goals
and has multiple benefits. The benefits include:
• Increases socializing and builds bonds
• Promotes connection and involvement to place
• Understanding of community challenges
While there appears to already be a shared understanding of Fairfield’s
obstacles among the residents, volunteering allows the residents to make
a difference and produce a sense of belonging. Volunteering allows the
solutions to stay inside the Fairfield community. Civic engagement can
maintain residents for the long-term stability of Fairfield.

DOWNTOWN IMPROVEMENTS
The Fairfield business district is major component of Fairfield’s identity.
The public engagement process revealed that while people love
downtown Fairfield, there are opportunities for improvement. One
way to improve the overall quality of life and community character
is to enhance the public spaces that encourage social interaction
within the downtown. The public spaces downtown can be improved
through additional seating, slower traffic, public art and streets trees /
plantings. When public spaces bring people into the downtown, they
have additional opportunities to shop and eat locally which in turn
creates a healthier and more vibrant downtown.
Fairfield should continue with on-going efforts to improve and update
the facades of historic downtown buildings. These efforts will improve
the appearance and character of the downtown district. Another
potential way to improve the downtown is to consider converting the
one-way streets to two-way streets. This process can often slow traffic
and increase pedestrian safety. The public should be encouraged to
slow down and experience the downtown area rather than speed by
it. The downtown district should aim to be walkable and accessible to
all users.

STREETSCAPE IMPROVEMENTS
Streetscape improvements such as sidewalk improvements, street
furniture, enhanced landscaping and street trees, should be
implemented throughout commercial and some residential districts.
Streetscape improvements can often be an opportunity for a city to
integrate green infrastructure features such as rain gardens. Using
green infrastructure such as permeable pavement, native plantings,
and street trees throughout the community to manage stormwater,
urban heat, and air quality is another chance for Fairfield to integrate
sustainable practices.

SIDEWALK IMPROVEMENT PROGRAM
Being able to make Fairfield streets walkable promotes a healthy lifestyle and
the safety of the community compared to other car-dependent communities.
Investing in a Sidewalk Improvement Program is essential to make Fairfield
safe for pedestrians and for connecting the residential, commercial, and
the downtown district together. Upkeeping neighborhood sidewalks will
ensure that the older neighborhoods are appealing for incoming residents
to purchase. As Fairfield experiences population growth, the City will need
to respond with investing in safe and welcoming sidewalks.

Location Quotient Analysis

The location quotient is the ratio of area concentration of industry employment
to the national average concentration. A location quotient that is greater
than one shows that the industry has a higher share of employment than
the national average. A location quotient that is less than one shows the
industry is less prevalent in the area than the national average. The higher
the location quotient, the more unlike the national average for employment
in the industry. This can often indicate a specialization for an area.
The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics has performed for major metropolitan
areas and regions in Iowa. While Fairfield is not large enough to warrant
its own analysis, there has been one completed for southeastern Iowa. In
southeast Iowa, the top three general industries with the highest location
quotients are:
• Production (LQ 2.71)
• Farming, Fishing & Forestry (LQ 1.52)
• Installation, Maintenance, and Repair (LQ 1.20)

Annexiation, 2-Mile Extra Territorial Review, & 2-Mile Zoning
To protect its growth area and ensure a diverse tax base, the City should
work with property owners in the County that are within the City’s growth
areas to agree to annex into the City prior to development. The City will
benefit by having an expanded tax base and the developing property will
benefit by having access to City infrastructure services allowing it to develop
at a greater density and value.
Conversely, the City should not approve subdivisions within its two-mile
extra territorial subdivision plat review authority that do not follow the
land use recommendations of the Comprehensive Plan and that are not
adequately served by public infrastructure including water, sanitary sewer,
and paved road access. City water and sanitary sewer service should only
be provided to properties within the corporate limits of Fairfield unless a
binding annexation agreement is executed with the property owners.
To promote the orderly and planned development outside the corporate limits
of Fairfield, the City may wish to explore establishing zoning authority within
the two-mile area outside the municipal boundary. This should be done in
collaboration with the County and the property owners in the area as a mutual
benefit to protect the interests and property values of all those involved.

TRANSPORTATION OVERVIEW
Transportation and mobility refer to the ways in which Fairfield residents and visitors move throughout the community. Mobility can come through multiple
modes of transport, many of which are available, in varying degrees, to Fairfield residents. Mode types include by car/van, walking, biking, transit, and
aviation. Fairfield’s shape and development pattern is largely defined by the major roadways that intersect the town: Highway 34 and Burlington Avenue.
In addition to automobile circulation, there are many Fairfield residents that rely on bikes or walking as their main form of transportation and this chapter
highlights the trail system available in and around Fairfield. A high-level discussion of the aviation and transit options available to residents is discussed
also. Finally, a series of proposed street extensions are recommended to complement the proposed future land use plan created in this report.

TRANSPORTATION & MOBILITY

ROADWAY CLASSIFICATION

Figure 12.1 Local, Collector, Minor Arterial & Major Arterial Examples

There are four main roadway classifications identified within Fairfield,
described below.

Major (Principal) Arterial
These roads carry the largest capacity of traffic over longer distances except
through city limits. Access is typically limited to other arterials and collector
roads with no access to individual properties or local roadways. No on-street
parking is allowed.

Minor Arterial
These roads carry slightly less traffic than major arterials at lower speeds (30
MPH to 40 MPH) but function the same by limiting access. Efficiency, speed,
and safety are the primary objectives. These routes are generally considered
truck routes for industry and connect rural roads to the city system.

Collector Streets
Collectors act as an efficient link to move traffic between arterial roadways
and local roadways. Speeds are typically lower than arterial routes (25
MPH to 35 MPH). Access is normally restricted to the primary entrances
to developments or residential neighborhoods. Parking may be allowed
on collector routes, but it is not advised due to higher speeds and traffic
volumes. These streets are generally through streets, transporting local
traffic efficiently.

Local Streets
Local Streets serve direct access to individual residential, commercial, or
industrial lots. The roads connect with collector roadways and are designed
to discourage through traffic movements. Speed is usually limited to 25 MPH
due to driveway spacing, on-street parking, shared use with bicycles, and
the presence of pedestrians.

TRANSPORTATION & MOBILITY

FREIGHT RAIL & HEAVY COMMERCIAL VEHICLE FACILITIES
According to the Fairfield Economic Development Association, Fairfield has
24 motor freight carriers that serve the community. Fairfield is bisected by
the Burlington Northern Santa Fe (BNSF) Railway north of the downtown.
There are rail spur-equipped sites located along or near the railway. Fairfield
is connected to a major four-lane highway, Highway 163/34. This corridor
provides Fairfield direct access to Burlington and Des Moines, Iowa, where
freight can connect with Interstates 80 and 35.

TRANSIT SERVICES
Fairfield is served by 10-15 Transit, a state and federally funded public
transportation system available to anyone needing transportation in
Region 15 and 17. These regions include the counties of Appanoose, David,
Jefferson, Keokuk, Lucas, Mahaska, Monroe, Van Buren, Wapello and Wayne.
The service is open to the general public including those with disabilities.
According to the service provider, riders may use the service for nearly any
purpose. In-town trips cost $2.00 and County trips cost $4.00. The service
operates 24 hours a day and 7 days a week, but trips must be scheduled at
least one business day in advance and are subject to availability of drivers
and vehicles.
Fairfield is also served by commercial buses through Jefferson Lines and
Burlington Trailways. The Amtrak California Zephyr line has two stops close
to Fairfield in nearby Ottumwa and Mount Pleasant, Iowa, both of which are
approximately 25 miles away.

AVITATION FACILITIES
The Fairfield Municipal Airport is a public airport located approximately three
miles northwest of the downtown at 2013 Airport Road, Fairfield, Iowa. It
is owned by the City of Fairfield. The airport is full-service, a fixed-base
operator and can provide 24-hour service if requested. Three hangars
are available for rent. A primary activity for the airport is its role as a cropdusting starting point from July 1 to September 1. The closest commercial
international commercial airport is in Cedar Rapids, Iowa.

Bicycle and pedestrian circulation within Fairfield are key considerations in
mobility planning. U.S. Census Data indicates that an above average number
of people bike or walk to work in Fairfield. Trails were a heavily supported
amenity by the public throughout the public participation exercises.

Fairfield Loop Trail
The Fairfield Loop Trail is a 15.9-mile project that acts as a beltway around
the City of Fairfield for pedestrians and bicyclists. Its path takes users on a
tour of several large parks throughout the community including Chautauqua
Park, Waterworks Park, Witham Woods and Jefferson County Park. There are
several designated bikeway / walkway routes that offer connection to the
trail from different areas of the community.

GLOSSARY OF TERMS
Environmentally and socially sustainable practices - refers to practices that promote both environmental and social equity in terms of the environment. This can
be accomplished through site design that mimics the natural cycle by limiting impermeable pavement or practices that do not unfairly put lower income residents
near areas with known pollutants or brownfields.
Agri-hoods - a housing development centered around community farming with housing built around repurposed or existing farmland. The farmland can provide
food, generate employment, provide recreation opportunities and promote neighborhood growth.
Greyfield sites - abandoned public buildings, industrial or commercial properties that are vacant, blighted, obsolete, or otherwise underutilized. The site has
infrastructure in place, but the current use is outdated or prevents a better or more efficient use of the property.
Very small-lot (tiny) homes - typically a dwelling that is 400 square feet or less in floor area and is not a loft.
Type 1 perennial streams - streams that water flowing year-round with water coming from smaller upstream waters or groundwater and are only supplemented by
runoff or rainfall.
Permaculture - plantings that are edible and meant to be sustainable and self-sufficient.
Complete Streets Policy & Program - refers to the simple and basic concept that streets and roadways should be designed, constructed and operated to be safe and
accessible for all transportation users whether they are pedestrians, bicyclists, transit riders, vehicular motorists or trucks. See appendix for Fairfield’s Complete Street Policy.
